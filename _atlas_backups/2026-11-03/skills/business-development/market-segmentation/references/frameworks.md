# Framework How-Tos

Run only the framework the decision needs. A framework that changes nothing should not be
in the memo. Defaults: **Porter** for segment attractiveness on entry, **PESTEL** when
regulation or macro risk shapes entry, **Ansoff** for growth planning, **SWOT** only as a
synthesis, **BCG** only for existing portfolios.

## Porter's Five Forces

**Use when:** judging whether a segment is structurally attractive (entry decision).
**Inputs:** competitor count and concentration, buyer concentration, supplier power,
substitute availability, barriers to entry (capital, licensing, switching costs).

Run it as a one-line verdict per force with the evidence behind the score:

| Force | Intensity (Low/Med/High) | Evidence |
|-------|--------------------------|----------|
| Rivalry | | How many credible players; price competition? |
| New entrants | | Capital, licensing, brand barriers |
| Substitutes | | What else solves the job, including "do nothing" |
| Buyer power | | Are buyers concentrated and price-savvy? |
| Supplier power | | Do you depend on a few suppliers/platforms? |

**Output:** an overall attractiveness statement and the two forces that most gate your entry.
**Trap:** treating it as a checklist of 5 bullets. It is an argument about whether profit is
structurally available; if every force is medium, you have said nothing.

## PESTEL

**Use when:** a market is regulated, or macro conditions (currency, politics, data law) can
kill entry. **Inputs:** the actual statutes, regulator guidance, currency and payment rails,
macro data.

Cover the six headings with a specific fact and a source each — not generic statements:
Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, Legal.

**Output:** the two or three external factors that are real constraints or real tailwinds,
each with a source. **Trap:** writing a news summary. Only keep items that change the entry
decision; delete the rest.

## SWOT

**Use when:** you already have findings and need to synthesize them for an audience.
**Inputs:** the outputs of Porter/PESTEL, product gaps, competitive evidence.

Fill four quadrants — Strengths, Weaknesses (internal), Opportunities, Threats (external).
**Output:** a synthesis slide. **Trap:** running SWOT first, as analysis. It is not analysis;
it is a summary of analysis done elsewhere. If a quadrant is empty, you have not done the
upstream work — do not invent entries to fill the box.

## BCG matrix

**Use when:** allocating investment across an **existing** product or segment portfolio.
Do not use it to evaluate a new market you do not yet serve.

Axes: market growth (y) vs relative market share (x). Classify each offering as star, cash
cow, question mark, or dog. **Output:** an allocation recommendation (invest, hold, harvest,
divest) per offering. **Trap:** applying it to greenfield segments — relative share is
undefined when you have no share.

## Ansoff growth matrix

**Use when:** enumerating growth options for planning. **Inputs:** your current product and
market set.

Four quadrants: market penetration (existing product, existing market), product development
(new product, existing market), market development (existing product, new market), and
diversification (new both). **Output:** a ranked list of growth options with the riskiest
called out. **Trap:** treating diversification as equally weighted; it carries the most risk
and usually the least right-to-win. Rank it last unless you can name the proof.

## Choosing between them

- Entry into a regulated market: PESTEL first (can we operate?), then Porter (is it worth it?).
- Choosing among segments: neither — use the scorecard. Frameworks describe one market; the
  scorecard compares several.
- Growth planning: Ansoff to enumerate, then score the options with the scorecard criteria.
- Presenting to a board: SWOT to summarize, sourced with the workings above.
