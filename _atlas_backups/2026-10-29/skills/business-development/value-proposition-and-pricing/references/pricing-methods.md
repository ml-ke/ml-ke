# Pricing Methods

Default to **value-based pricing** with a metric tied to value. Cost-plus and
competitor-matching are fallbacks, not defaults — they anchor you to a cost base or a
rival's model and leave value on the table.

## The three approaches

| Approach | Set price by | Use it when | Trap |
|----------|--------------|-------------|------|
| Value-based | the quantified value to the buyer | you can measure value (default) | needs evidence; do the ROI first |
| Cost-plus | cost to serve + margin | commoditized, cost is the buyer's frame | caps you at cost + margin forever |
| Competitor-based | what rivals charge | parity markets, price is the axis | race to the bottom; their model, not yours |

Recommendation: build the value case (see the ROI outline), then use competitor pricing only
to frame the range, and cost only to set the floor.

## Choosing the price metric

The metric is the unit you charge by. The right metric meets four tests:

1. **Scales with value** — the buyer pays more as they get more.
2. **Easy to count** — both sides can verify it without dispute.
3. **Predictable** — the buyer can forecast the bill; unpredictable bills kill renewals.
4. **Aligned to how they already budget** — per seat, per location, per transaction, per outcome.

Common metrics: per user/seat, per usage unit (transaction, API call, payout), flat platform
fee, or a hybrid (platform fee + usage). Default to the metric that grows as the customer's
value grows, and resist switching metrics mid-contract.

## Tier design (good-better-best)

1. Anchor the **middle tier** to your ICP's most common need; make it the obvious choice.
2. Fence tiers with capabilities the buyer **naturally groups** — a real step in their job,
   not a feature removed to create a gap.
3. Price the top tier as a multiple (commonly 2-3x) of the middle, justified by delivered
   value, not by feature count.
4. Give every tier a **named buyer** ("for a 30-bed clinic", "for a multi-branch network")
   so the choice is obvious.

## Discount policy and guardrails

Write the policy, or every call reprices the product. State:

- **What is discountable** (term, seats, onboarding) and **what is not** (statutory or
  compliance features the buyer must have regardless).
- **What every discount is traded for:** prepayment, longer term, a case study, volume
  commitment, or a reference. A discount with no trade is a leak.
- **Approval thresholds:** which discounts a rep may approve, and which escalate. Put the
  floor price in writing (coordinate with bd-context section 7).
- **Prepayment model:** the discount or fee waiver offered for annual upfront payment.

## Willingness-to-pay research

Weakest to strongest signal:

1. **Win/loss and churn analysis** — did price cause the outcome, or something else? Ask.
2. **Competitor anchors** — what the alternative costs (coordinate with competitive-intelligence).
3. **Customer interviews** — direct WTP questions across a price range.
4. **Structured surveys** — Van Westendorp (price sensitivity meter) or Gabor-Granger for a
   defensible range. Conjoint trade-off analysis if you need to price features together.

Record the evidence per price point. An untested price is a hypothesis — label it.

## Traps

1. **Discounting to close instead of repackaging.** If everyone needs a discount, the tier
   design or the price metric is wrong.
2. **Price metric that punishes growth.** A metric that makes the buyer feel taxed for
   expanding (e.g. per-order fees on a growing volume) drives them off the platform.
3. **Feature-shaving as fencing.** Arbitrary gaps invite negotiation on every line.
4. **Copying a competitor's price without their cost structure.** You inherit their margins.
5. **Free-forever anchoring.** A permanent free tier can cap what the market will pay unless
   it is a deliberate funnel.
6. **Changing metric mid-relationship.** It breaks budgets and trust; grandfather existing
   accounts and migrate slowly if you must.
