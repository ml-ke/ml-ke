## 1. What was learned (sources + reputability)

### 1.1 Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" has moved on since the humanizer fork — HIGH reputability
Source: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing> (WikiProject AI Cleanup; cites NYT
2025-12-03 and arxiv 2508.01930). The page is *advice*, not policy, but it is the canonical community
catalogue and it is maintained continuously — whereas the `blader/humanizer` fork it was ported from is static.

Three concrete divergences, all verified by reading the live page:
1. **"Overuse of em dashes" is now a HISTORICAL indicator**, filed with the range **(2022–September 2026)**.
   Verbatim caveat: *"This sign is most useful when taken in combination with other indicators, not by itself."*
   Our humanizer presented it as active pattern #14 with a flat "LLMs use em dashes more than humans" claim.
2. **A better discriminator exists: spacing.** AI em dashes are usually **surrounded by spaces**
   (`word — word`); common human typographic practice is unspaced (`word—word`). We were counting all dashes.
3. **An explicit "Ineffective indicators" section** (perfect grammar; mixing casual and formal registers)
   plus gaps in the word lists: `bolstered`, `deep dive`, `meticulous/meticulously`, `robust` were missing
   from the vocabulary list; `functions as`, `operates as`, `maintains` from the copula list.

This matters beyond prose: **we publish posts about AI detection.** Over-claiming a retired indicator is a
factual error in our own content — and it matches our corpus-sweep lesson that over-flagging is the more
dangerous failure ("a false flag makes real content get rewritten").

### 1.2 arxiv 2604.02837 — *Towards Secure Agent Skills* (Zhiyuan Li, Jingzheng Wu, Xiang Ling, Xing Cui, Tianyue Luo) — HIGH
First comprehensive security analysis of the Agent Skills framework. Full lifecycle in four phases
(Creation → Distribution → Deployment → Execution), a threat taxonomy of **7 categories / 17 scenarios
across 3 attack layers**, validated against **5 confirmed real incidents**.

Headline finding: *"the most severe threats arise from structural properties of the framework itself,
including the **absence of a data-instruction boundary**, a **single-approval persistent trust model**,
and the **lack of mandatory marketplace security review** — and cannot be addressed through incremental
mitigations alone."*

The middle one is directly actionable: a reviewed skill is trusted forever while its bytes change. A
recorded content hash is the countermeasure, and we had manifests with hashes sitting right there.

### 1.3 arxiv 2607.13987 — *SkillSec-Eval* (Sanket Badhe, Priyanka Tiwari) — HIGH
Lifecycle-aware security framework; threat taxonomy spanning **repository admission → semantic retrieval →
planner selection → execution → skill evolution**, evaluated on **327 real-world skills**. Conclusion:
vulnerabilities arise at stages *beyond* execution.

Gap analysis against our pipeline: admission = scanner + Step 6 triage ✓; retrieval = 60-char description
routing + semantic-attack research ✓; execution = capability-chain reading ✓; evolution = bridge stamping +
seam check + (new) provenance ✓. **Planner selection is the thin one** — which of several loaded skills the
planner picks. Step 7 regime gating covers name/alias collisions, not selection bias. Watch item.

### 1.4 arxiv 2608.10906 — *GitSkills* (Destefanis/UCL, Graziotin/Hohenheim, Ortu & Vaccargiu/Cagliari) — HIGH
Dataset of **3,797,117 SKILL.md files from 282,200 public repositories** (July 2026), grouped into
**1,877,981 distinct contents**. So **~50.6% of every SKILL.md occurrence on GitHub is a byte-identical copy
of another one.** No central registry, no package manager: *"developers reuse them by copying folders between
repositories."* Shipped as one self-contained SQLite file.

This is the population-scale version of exactly what the seam and provenance checks caught this run — a
library whose copies drift silently because copying *is* the distribution mechanism. It also gives us a
defensible number for the claim (previously asserted qualitatively).

### 1.5 Brazil — Fabio Akita, "Falando um pouco sobre minhas Skills de IA" (2026-09-17) — MEDIUM, high convergence
<https://akitaonrails.com/2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia/>

The recommendation opens the article: **"não use as skills dos outros"** — don't use other people's skills.
His published repo says "not tailored for general usage"; the point is to read them for the *reasoning* and
structure, then build your own. *"Copiar skill de terceiro e sair usando é o equivalente digital de assinar um
contrato sem ler. Pior: é instalar as instruções de outra pessoa pra rodar com as suas permissões, nos seus
repositórios, com os seus tokens. Prompt injection você instala voluntariamente e agradece."*

Two more claims worth keeping:
- **"Skill encostada é dívida"** — an idle skill is debt: you change process, the skill ages, and one day the
  agent executes a rule that no longer applies. "Less skills, better written, reviewed often."
- He cites Anthropic's engineering position that *"more tools don't always lead to better outcomes"* — few,
  well-thought tools for high-impact workflows.

A personal blog is not evidence, but every claim converges with our primary sources: arxiv 2603.21019
(>90% of high-download skills fail strict audit), 2608.08453 (91.8% carry ≥1 defect), 2608.12610 (<100
reliable trigger slots). Recorded as design validation, not fact.
