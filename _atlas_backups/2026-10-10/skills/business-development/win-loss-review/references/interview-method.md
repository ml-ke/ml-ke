# Win/Loss Interview Method

Detail for Steps 3 and 4 of `win-loss-review`. Load this when running the buyer and internal debriefs.

## Rule zero: who is in the room

Interview the buyer without the rep. A buyer will not tell a rep's face that they disliked the rep, distrusted the price, or preferred a competitor's roadmap. Run the buyer interview as a neutral, off-the-record conversation, ideally by someone who was not on the deal. Promise nothing in return for candour, and state how the answer will be used (anonymised, in aggregate).

## Where the review sits in the timeline

Interview the buyer 2-6 weeks after the decision:

- Too soon: they still owe you politeness and the decision is fresh and defended.
- Too late: they have forgotten the detail and rationalised the choice.

For wins, interview at the same cadence — win interviews reveal the differentiator and often the real reason they bought, which is rarely the reason you sold.

## The buyer interview

### Open

"Thanks for the time. This is not a sales call — we are trying to learn, and nothing here is attributed to you by name. I will ask what actually drove the decision, then dig into specifics."

### Core questions

1. Walk me through how the decision was made, from when you started looking to the final call.
2. Who was involved, and who had the final say?
3. What were the two or three things that mattered most in choosing?
4. How did you weigh us against the alternatives — including doing nothing?
5. What almost changed your mind? What was the closest call?
6. What did you tell your boss, or your board, when you recommended this?
7. If you were advising a peer in the same position, what would you say about us?
8. What would have made this an easier decision?
9. What happens now if this works, and what happens if it does not?

### For known losses

- What did the winner have that we did not?
- At what point did you decide against us?
- Was there a moment we could have changed the outcome?
- What would we have had to do to win?

### Listen for and capture verbatim

- The words they use for their problem and for your product.
- The point in time the decision actually flipped.
- Objections they never voiced to the rep.
- Whether the outcome was the economic buyer's decision or the committee's.

Do not argue, correct, or defend. You are there to record, not to relitigate the deal.

## The internal debrief

### Open

Set the frame: "This is about the system, not the person. We are looking for what our process missed, not who to blame." Blamelessness is not politeness — a rep who expects blame will shade the record and the review becomes worthless.

### Questions

1. At each stage gate, what did we know and what did we assume?
2. Who did we think the economic buyer was, and were we right?
3. What did the buyer tell us that we did not act on?
4. Where did we lose access, momentum, or the narrative?
5. What did we believe that turned out to be wrong?
6. What would we do differently at the gate that mattered?
7. What did not exist — a proof point, a feature, a reference — that we needed?

### Capture disagreements, do not settle them

Where the rep and the SE, or the rep and the data, disagree, write both down. A disagreement about what happened is itself a finding: it means the deal record is unreliable in that spot.

## Reconciling the three accounts

After both interviews, lay them side by side:

| Question | Buyer said | Rep believed | Data shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| What decided it | | | |
| Who decided it | | | |
| When it flipped | | | |

The gaps are the output. A rep who believed trust won and a buyer who said price decided it is a coaching and messaging finding, not a scoreboard.

## Ethics

- Quote the buyer only with permission; anonymise by default.
- Never pressure a buyer to participate, and never imply a commercial consequence for declining.
- Never fold a single buyer's opinion into a claim about "the market".
- Keep the raw interview notes separate from the synthesis so a claim can be traced to its source.
